# Allied nuclear deterrence and forward deployment

The nuclear alliance model separates four decisions that are often conflated:

1. **Consultation** — strategic consultation/signalling without a nuclear guarantee.
2. **Extended deterrence** — a nuclear-capable provider can include an ally or allied territory inside selected nuclear red lines without stationing nuclear assets there.
3. **Basing rights / forward deployment** — the host can consent to bombers, strategic land missiles or strategic submarines being deployed to a specific allied region. Peacetime, temporary and crisis basing are separately authorised.
4. **Nuclear sharing** — a deeper arrangement that can combine shared planning with basing rights. It still does not transfer ownership or launch authority in this model.

Red-line categories are explicit and independently configurable. A provider may, for example, protect an ally from nuclear attack and invasion while leaving lesser conventional attacks deliberately ambiguous. Public guarantees are more legible to opponents than secret understandings.

Forward deployment is not required for a guarantee, but visible deployed assets increase the credibility and escalation signal. This permits Cuban-Missile-Crisis-style deployments to emerge from ordinary alliance, basing and crisis mechanics.

Host consent is authoritative. Revocation immediately prevents new deployments and suspends extended-deterrence coverage until consent is restored. Deployments are persistent records tied to a particular host region, so different territories of the same allied polity can have different basing arrangements.

Extended deterrence feeds the arms-race/non-proliferation model as a security assurance. A credible umbrella can reduce the pressure for an ally to build an independent deterrent; weak or withdrawn coverage can reverse that incentive.
